Reflection

My own take on how the agent team did. Items marked from the run history are details added from the audit trail so I don't forget them.

How each ticket went

Ticket 102 (rent) was the most straightforward, and the agents did well on it. Every run followed the same path: Facilities checked the lease, Accounting prepared the $2,400 payment, and Customer Service drafted the reply.

Ticket 101 (tee) depended on a second item: invoice 501 had to be paid before the vendor would ship anything. That was a little unclear from a single run, but it was easy to work out after running the agents more than once.

Ticket 103 (hoodies) is where we had to change things. We adjusted the instructions, and we added a button so a person can resolve the ticket manually, because there isn't enough cash to let the agents finish it on their own.

from the run history The money explains why 103 can't resolve itself. Everything this shop needed to spend came to $3,512.00: rent $2,400 + invoice $840 + the $8 tee order + a $264 restock for the 12 missing hoodies. Checking started at $3,400.00, so after the first three items ($3,248.00) only $152.00 was left, which is $112.00 short of the hoodie restock. Pending approvals also hold cash, so the agents see that shortfall as soon as an earlier ticket has a proposal waiting.

What I expected vs. what happened

  • Ticket 101: the boss called the agents in a different order than I expected. I had Accounting first; it called Inventory first and then Accounting. The part I did predict, Accounting asking Inventory itself, did happen.
  • The boss had a bigger role than I expected on every ticket. It had to check who to delegate to next in between steps, instead of just handing off and waiting.
  • Ticket 102: I thought Accounting would go on to use pay_approved, and it never did. My understanding of what pay_approved does was slightly off. It is the step that actually moves money, so it is human-only: the dashboard's Approve button calls it, and no agent can. Accounting did use propose_payment as I expected.
  • Ticket 103: the boss delegated to Accounting first instead of Inventory, to ask about margin and discount ceilings. That turned out not to be very useful, because we hadn't actually set any discount rules. The agents did a good job of flagging exactly that, and it led to the new version of the flow, where I make the judgment call at the end and mark the ticket resolved myself.

from the run history How much the boss did shows up in the numbers. Across the final runs the boss made 5 of 15 model requests on 101, 5 of 12 on 102, and 4 of 10 on 103, roughly a third to 42% of the team's model requests. Over the 17 successful runs, the pattern was very steady: 102 always went Facilities, then Accounting, then Customer Service (5 of 5 runs), and 103 always went Accounting, then Inventory, then Customer Service (5 of 5). Only 101 changed over time: Accounting started asking Inventory itself in the later runs, and in one early run only the invoice was prepared and the $8 order was missed until I tightened the boss and accounting instructions.

What I would change

I would merge some of the Accounting agent's tools and capabilities into the boss. The boss had to check with Accounting before almost every delegation, and mostly it wanted a number: the cash balance or a discount ceiling. If the boss could check the ceilings and cash balance directly, Accounting could focus on the rest of its job, preparing payments and purchase orders. That would remove a delegation round-trip on tickets like 103, where Accounting was only asked for numbers and prepared nothing.

from the run history The tools involved are read-only, get_cash_and_open_invoices and get_margin_for_discount, so giving them to the boss would not weaken the rule that only humans move money. The proposing tools should stay with Accounting.

Three new problems the agents could be used for

  1. Pay utility bills. Another kind of invoice, handled the same way: Accounting prepares it and a human approves it.
  2. Proactively flag where more stock will be needed soon, for example when stock falls below a set threshold. Inventory would watch the levels instead of waiting for a ticket.
  3. Source vendors to proactively add more stock. Inventory could go beyond the three vendors in the table and look for new ones.

Three problems the agents can't solve right now

  1. Predicting demand for specific items with any statistical rigor. We would need historical sales data and statistical tools for the agents to use.
  2. Sending automatic emails when customers ask about items that are clearly out of stock. We would need to connect the agents to email. Right now they can only draft.
  3. Making judgment calls on bulk discounts. As we saw with ticket 103, the agents can't set discount rates without rules. If we gave them rules for bulk discounts (for example, price at $X for every Y orders) and allowed them to approve new prices dynamically, they could.

from the run history The first and third gaps share a cause: the agents can only be as good as the data and rules we give them. On 103 they stopped at "no discount policy exists" and said so, which is the right behavior until a policy is written.

Other things to remember from the runs

  • from the run history The instructions took real iteration. Early replies leaked internal details (vendor invoices, "awaiting approval") to customers, the boss sometimes skipped the customer reply, and the first run failed outright because the model needed a different API route. Each was a prompt or setup fix, not a bigger model.
  • from the run history Repeat runs from a clean database were identical. Three separate runs of ticket 101 had exactly the same steps and token counts, and two of them took about 6 seconds against about 25 for the first. That looks like cached model answers, though I didn't confirm it. It is handy for repeatable tests, but it means a repeat run from the same state doesn't show how much the agents vary.
  • from the run history A real run costs about 10 to 15 model requests and 14k to 22k input tokens per ticket, so the final run of all three tickets was about 54k tokens in and 3k out (22,428 + 17,475 + 14,167 in; 1,357 + 916 + 950 out).
  • from the run history Order of approvals matters. In the final run I approved the $8 order before invoice 501 was paid, and the payment was refused twice until the invoice went through. The dashboard now shows why on the card.
